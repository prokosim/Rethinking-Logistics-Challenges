## Project

The case is a large steel-frame warehouse in Breiðholt, Reykjavík, about 11,000 m² in footprint and later expanded to over 14,000 m². It stands 14 m from apartment blocks and drew strong public backlash for blocking views and daylight. The student identifies two problems: the 'big box' is sized for peak demand, so in Iceland's cold climate much of it is heated while empty, and it creates a dead zone in a mixed-use neighbourhood. Her proposal is an 'elastic' building: movable partition walls and foldable scissor racks shrink the active, heated storage zone off-peak and free the dormant area for galleries, markets and events. An openable timber façade and a hybrid pneumatic ETFE roof let the building switch between a closed, compact logistics mode and an open, daylit civic mode. An appendix adds native Icelandic planting (birch, rowan, willow, yarrow, cottongrass), bioswales and a restored pond.

## How the student framed the challenge

- Urban integration of logistics buildings

## Trade-offs

- Maximising green roof coverage conflicts with minimising structural cost and embodied carbon (questionnaire).
- An open façade for public permeability and daylight conflicts with the thermal performance needed in compact, closed logistics mode, so the façade switches between the two modes.
- Robotic partitions reconfigure much faster (5–15 min vs 2–4 h) and need less labour, but cost more to operate and have higher environmental impact than manual partitions.
- A fully pneumatic envelope gives the most spatial flexibility, while a hybrid with only the roof inflatable trades some of that for structural stability.

## Decision

The energy estimate supported adaptive thermal zoning with manual movable partitions and foldable racks, so the active heated zone shrinks off-peak and the dormant area goes to public use. Comparisons of façade and roof options led to an openable timber façade and a hybrid pneumatic ETFE roof chosen for flexibility, lower structural weight and daylight.
